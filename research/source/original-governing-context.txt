The governing principle for all of this: treat both papers as forks, not inspiration. The scientific value of what you're doing comes entirely from changing exactly one thing — soft reweighting → hard exclusion + restart — while holding every other knob (base model, reward function, dataset, eval metric, even the prompts) identical to theirs. Every extra change you make "while you're in there" (better clustering, different base model, new eval) reopens the question of what actually caused any difference you see. The discipline of *not* improving things is more important than the blocking mechanism itself.

## Track A — Uniqueness-Aware RL ("Rewarding the Rare"), do this first

**Steal wholesale, don't redesign:** the three-stage judge-clustering prompts are printed verbatim in their Appendix A — use them exactly as written. The reward functions for math/physics/medicine are also given exactly (boxed-answer match, numeric tolerance, LLM-judge y/n). Don't touch any of it. More importantly: they hand you two AIME problems with a fully worked, human-verified taxonomy of alternate solutions already built — `aime24_i_p10` (5 canonical methods: Power of Point + Law of Cosines, Symmedian Similarity, Three Tangents Lemma, Ptolemy + chord ratio, pure trigonometry) and `aime2025_ii_p3` (4 methods: binary constraint formulation, interior-segments casework, trail/flow viewpoint, dynamic programming), complete with derivations. Building a taxonomy like that from scratch is the expensive part of their whole paper (manual normalization of 3-5 human write-ups per problem). You get two for free. Start there before doing anything else — it means your first result costs you zero taxonomy-building labor.

**The actual swap, and why it's the right one:** their mechanism is Eq. 4-5 — advantage gets multiplied by `1/f^α`, continuous, computed fresh within every batch of K rollouts. Two changes turn this into your method: (1) hard threshold instead of continuous decay — once a cluster's share crosses some τ, zero its members rather than discount them; (2) make cluster membership *persistent across training steps* rather than recomputed from scratch every batch. That second change isn't a stylistic preference — it's directly motivated by a limitation the authors state themselves: their method "measures rarity only within the rollout set of a single problem and does not explicitly capture long-term novelty... during training." Extending exactly that gap is the most defensible, least-arbitrary version of your idea, because you're not inventing a new criticism of their method, you're building the fix to the one they already named.

**What not to do:** don't try to match their full sweep (3 domains × 3 model families) before you've validated the mechanism. Get the two-problem result first.

**What to pay attention to, because it recalibrates what "success" means here:** on their own cover@n evaluation, the baseline and their trained model tied on 16 of 20 problems — their explicit diversity mechanism only moved the needle on 4/20. That's the real bar. If hard blocking also mostly does nothing, that's not a failed experiment, that's consistent with how hard this actually is; the interesting question is whether you beat 4/20, not whether you solve coverage outright. Also watch their group size — K=8 rollouts is a noisy sample for an LLM judge to cluster reliably; a dominant-cluster call made off 8 samples in one batch is shaky, which is another argument for aggregating your dominance signal across steps rather than trusting one snapshot.

**One thing to carry over unchanged:** their reweighting only ever touches *correct* rollouts — incorrect ones stay penalized regardless of cluster size, so the mechanism can never accidentally reward being wrong-but-different. Your hard version needs the identical guardrail. The failure mode to design against is blocking the *only* correct cluster on a hard problem and deleting your entire learning signal for that problem. Restart, don't amputate.

## Track B — STP, do this second, once Track A has told you the mechanism works

**Steal wholesale:** the self-play skeleton (conjecturer proposes, prover attempts, verifier gates both), the seed-lemma-conditioned conjecturing format, and — this is the one worth noting for your "continuous reward" question from last time — their actual reward isn't purely binary even though the terminal check is: on top of correct/incorrect, they multiply in a length discount (γ^L) and, for Lean specifically, a verification-time discount (β^T), so shorter and faster proofs score strictly higher than slow, bloated ones. That's a real, working instance of the binary-gate-plus-continuous-shaping pattern, already implemented, in code you can read.

**The gift already sitting in this paper:** STP hit mode collapse in the conjecturer during early experiments — generated conjectures skewed toward algebra even when seed statements were number theory — and their fix was a Wasserstein-distance reprojection that pushes the conjecture distribution toward matching the topic balance of the unsolved statement pool. That is a soft, already-built, already-justified diversity mechanism sitting exactly where you'd want to swap in hard blocking. You don't need to argue mode collapse is real in this setting — they already documented it and already shipped a fix for it. Your job is a direct ablation: does hard-blocking-and-restart beat their Wasserstein reprojection, on the same conjecturer.

But notice what their fix actually balances: *topic*, not *strategy*. It makes sure you don't get all-algebra conjectures — it says nothing about whether every proof within a topic reuses the same three tactics. That's the axis you're actually targeting, and it's orthogonal to what they already fixed, which is exactly the kind of gap you want — not "redo what they did," but "diversify along the dimension their existing mechanism doesn't touch."

**Reuse their restart machinery, repurpose the trigger:** STP already restarts — twice, in two different ways. A "periodic refresh" mid-training (retrain from the base model on all correct proofs so far, reset the replay buffer, restart self-play) that fires on a fixed schedule to prevent forgetting; and a separate "final re-training" at the end. Neither is triggered by detecting a dominant mode — they're both scheduled. Your contribution is changing the trigger from *schedule* to *event*: fire the same refresh machinery when a dominant tactic/lemma signature is detected, not every N iterations.

**Also reuse, and extend, their existing blocklist:** for the Isabelle track they already hard-disallow specific tactics (`sledgehammer`, `mason`, `smt`, `metis`, `sos`) by string-matching the proof and rejecting it outright if any appear — for compute reasons, not diversity ones, but it's the exact mechanism you want, already implemented, already validated at scale. For the Lean track, notably, they explicitly allow everything. That's your opening: introduce dynamic tactic/lemma blocking on the Lean side, where they currently have none, using the same rejection-by-substring-match approach they already trust on the Isabelle side.

**What not to do, and this is the important one:** don't try to match their scale. 241M generated proofs, 51.3B tokens, 48 iterations, a 32-node TPU-v4 pod. That's not a target, it's a distraction — a mechanism-isolation study doesn't need SOTA numbers, it needs a controlled comparison, and you can run that on a few hundred LeanWorkbook statements or just miniF2F-valid with a fraction of their K. Second, and this one will actually burn you if you miss it: they manually audited 20 randomly sampled "unproved" LeanWorkbook statements and found only 7 are actually provable — 13 are false or ill-posed due to autoformalization noise from the natural-language translation. If you interpret "blocking forced the model to fail to find an alternate proof" as a meaningful result without first checking the statement isn't simply garbage, you'll spend real effort chasing artifacts. Build a provability sanity pass before you trust any negative result on this dataset.

**Also reuse their reduced environment, because it happens to help you specifically:** for compute reasons, they import only the miniF2F-lean4 tactic set for the Lean track rather than the whole of mathlib. That's a small, bounded vocabulary — which is exactly what makes "detect the dominant tactic, add it to a blocklist, restart" tractable to implement and easy to audit. Don't "upgrade" to full mathlib for your version; the constraint that made their compute budget work also makes your bookkeeping problem smaller.

**The bar their own results set for you:** their worked examples of generated conjectures (Section 4.5) are mostly parametric generalizations — a fixed constant becomes a variable, an exponent gets bumped, the underlying proof technique is unchanged. That's what "diversity" currently looks like under their existing Wasserstein mechanism. If your hard-blocking variant *also* only produces parametric variants, you haven't shown anything beyond what they already get for free — the result you actually want is a structurally different lemma path, not the same proof with a different letter in it.

## Two things to keep straight across both tracks

These papers represent genuinely different notions of "unique," and conflating them in a write-up will undercut both results. Track A is *within-problem coverage of a known human taxonomy* — you're checking whether the model can be made to find things people already know how to do. Track B is *across-problem frontier expansion via self-generated conjectures* — genuinely open-ended, much harder to evaluate, no ground truth to check against beyond "is it absent from the training set." Keep the claims separate: Track A gives you a controlled, legible, defensible number; Track B gives you something closer to what you originally wanted but with much weaker evaluation guarantees.

And relatedly — in Track A, "blocking" is necessarily a reward-level or prompt-level intervention, since there's no formal action space to restrict; the policy could in principle ignore a prompt constraint, so you need a compliance check. In Track B, blocking is enforced by the harness itself via rejection of the generated proof — unroutable, no compliance-checking needed. That's a real methodological difference between the two tracks, not just a detail, and it's worth stating plainly in whatever you write up rather than letting "blocking" quietly mean two different strengths of constraint.
